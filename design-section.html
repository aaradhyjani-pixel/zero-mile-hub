The idea

Nagpur sits at India's geographic centre, marked by the Zero Mile stone. Put the country's transfer hub here and almost every domestic city is under two hours away. Atlanta's runway logic makes it work, and a fluid single roof makes it a place.

Runways

Six east-west runways in two triples either side of a 3.6 km midfield. Outer and middle runways are 1,550 m apart, so both can take independent instrument approaches (ICAO needs 1,525 m). Middle and inner runways are 850 m apart, enough for segregated operations (760 m). By default three runways land and three take off: 3 × 40 + 3 × 48 is about 264 movements an hour, just over the 260 design peak. The 4,500 m inner runway, 27L, handles heavy jets on 47 °C afternoons.

End-around taxiways at both ends let aircraft from the outer runways reach the terminal without crossing an active runway. Traffic flows in one-way loops: arrivals go west and around the end of the runways, departures go east. Head-on conflicts on the taxiways disappear.

The terminal

The Zero Mile Hall is a seven-lobed shell, like the segments of a Nagpur orange. Each lobe is a check-in and security hall. An oculus lights the Zero Mile marker in the floor, the one point every departing passenger crosses. From the Hall, the Mall runs east: a waisted spine, widest where each of the six concourses meets it, with the people mover underneath.

Concourses A to C are domestic, D swings between domestic and international, and E and F are international, with A380 gates at the pier ends. The roof has many small skylights instead of a few big ones, deep overhangs and a reflective membrane, so the building stays cool in the heat. The apron north and south of the piers is held in reserve for longer piers in phase 2.

Phasing

Bengaluru's traffic moves in first, on two runways and concourses A and B. Hyderabad adds two runways and concourse C. Mumbai brings the Gulf wave, the widebodies and five runways. Delhi completes the hub. The four game levels follow this order.
